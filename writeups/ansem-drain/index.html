Ansem's drained $2.57M didn't sit still. I traced it to the last coin.

On 24 November a wallet tied to Ansem dumped 87.5M $ANSEM. He says his Bullpen account was hacked. Critics say he sold himself. I followed every dollar across four chains. The trail does not stop at $2.57M: a day later the same receiving address took in another $1.58M on Ethereum. This is where all of it ended up, and what the chain can and cannot tell us.

The exit

At 14:08 Lookonchain noted the money had not moved. Less than four hours later it was gone, in two routes.

Route A. 1,066,426 USDC went straight from 0xAC33 into BridgersSwap, a cross-chain swap service, in five swaps.

Route B. 1,500,000 USDC went to three fresh wallets, 500,000 each. Each passed it into BridgersSwap in three chunks, within 9 minutes of receiving it. They share the same EIP-7702 delegate contract as 0xAC33, and this hop is their only USDC history.

Into Bitcoin

BridgersSwap writes the destination chain and address into each swap's input data as plain text. Decoding all 14 USDC swaps gives the same answer every time: Bitcoin. Route A paid out to one address, and each Route B wallet to its own.

The main Bitcoin exit address received its first deposits at 04:42:50, four hours and fourteen minutes before the first $ANSEM sale.

Whoever ran this had the Bitcoin side ready in advance. That fits a planned theft. It also fits a planned sale.

Those first deposits came from a taproot peel chain: eight addresses, each funded hours earlier, each paying a small amount to bc1qhvzh and passing the rest to a fresh address. At 13:54 another 1.59 BTC arrived from 12zacgJDkFf4GF7sqq83vRLFhENeRX3Vzh, a wallet active since 14 November. On day two all of it was swept together with the swap money, so one owner controls both.

Day two: one address

On 25 November, between 16:47 and 17:11 UTC, all four Bitcoin addresses were emptied. Every coin went to the same new taproot address. That includes the 1.63 BTC that sat on the main exit address before the swaps, so whoever controls the swap money controls that too.

The second wave: $1.58M on Ethereum

An address on Arbitrum is also an address on Ethereum. 0xAC33 was first used on Ethereum on 25 November at 00:01 UTC. Between 03:16 and 03:31 it received 1,578,932 USDC from a single active wallet, in 24 transfers. The first was a $10 test.

That payer, 0x3173, signed every transfer itself, kept working normally afterwards and still holds funds. It has 392 transactions and moves hundreds of thousands of dollars a day between a handful of counterparties. That is how an OTC desk or broker paying a client looks. It is not how a drained victim looks. If it is a desk, it knows who it paid.

Four minutes later the money was split: 500,000 to 0x0982, 500,000 to 0xc7c7 and 579,000 to 0x50a6. Each passed it to one more fresh wallet, which swapped it to ETH through a PMM swap contract. The ETH came back to the same three wallets.

The first $543,837: cash, taken two hours early

Every report starts this story with the selling. The chain starts it earlier. At 06:46:32 UTC the "ansemconzimp" wallet sent out 515,278 USDC, and seven seconds later 404.67 SOL. Both went to a wallet that had never been used. The wallet's own key signed both transfers.

The money then sat still for 66 minutes while the route was tested with pocket change: 0.39 SOL went in at one end and 27 USDC came out at 0xAC33. At 07:52 a $1 test followed, then the full amount.

The route was the same each time: a fresh Solana wallet, another fresh Solana wallet, a Relay bridge to a fresh Arbitrum wallet, and a signed USDC permit that let one relayer, 0xb711, pull the money into 0xAC33 one second later. Five throwaway wallets on two chains to move one wallet's cash.

The route ran through an app

Relay keeps a public record of every bridge. For these transfers it shows an app fee paid to 0xb711: 2% on the 0.39 SOL test, 0.5% on the 404.67 SOL and 0.01% on the 515,278 USDC. A fee scale like that belongs to a wallet or trading app, and 0xb711 is that app's fee wallet. The same address then acted as the relayer for the permits.

The Solana side agrees. The fresh wallets did not pay their own network fees. One fee payer, 5zDAX7NB, co-signed for them, and it co-signed for the old wallet GYfEZvZT as far back as May 2026. So this part of the operation was run from inside an app, using that app's sponsored transactions, its bridge and its relayer.

That fits Bullpen's statement that legacy beta accounts were taken over. The old wallet dates from April 2024, the start of Bullpen's beta. It does not prove which app it was, because neither address carries a public label.

The token sales were different. Their proceeds went straight from the selling wallet to 0xAC33 through Relay in blocks of 2,000 SOL, with no app fee.

Two readings, same data

Fits a theft

  • Bullpen confirmed unauthorized transfers on 18-19 early beta accounts, Ansem's included.Reported
  • The liquid cash was taken first, two hours before the token sales, and the route was tested with $27 and $1 before the large amounts.Onchain
  • The early transfers ran through an app's sponsored transactions, bridge and relayer, as an account takeover inside that app would.Onchain
  • Fresh wallets, split amounts and a fast swap into Bitcoin.Onchain
  • A second Ansem-linked wallet holding about 49% of $ANSEM supply did not move.Reported

Fits a staged sale

  • MLM says an old wallet he had tagged as Ansem's funded the receiver an hour before the selling.Reported
  • The first $543,837 at the receiver was the selling wallet's own cash, sent by its own key.Onchain
  • The Bitcoin exit was live more than four hours before the first sale.Onchain
  • Ansem co-founded Bullpen, so its confirmation is not independent.Reported
  • A day later an active wallet paid the same address $1.58M, starting with a $10 test. Payments like that usually go to a known counterparty.Onchain

The chain shows which key signed. It does not show whose hand was on it. A real theft and a staged one would leave this same trail.

  4. Who sent the 04:42 deposits to bc1qhvzh?
  5. Has anyone asked Bridgers to freeze or flag the swaps?

Method and sources

  • Amounts and times were read directly from the chains: Arbitrum (public RPC, USDC transfer logs and transaction input), Solana (public RPC, token balance changes of the selling wallet) and Bitcoin (mempool.space).
  • The "877 sells across 124 tokens" figure is Arkham's and was not recounted here.
  • Bridge origins and app fees come from Relay's public request records. Solana transfers were read from the chain.
  • Bullpen has said a single account supplied 99.8% of the stolen funds, as reported by The Crypto Times and others on 25 November.
  • Ethereum amounts were read from USDC, USDT and WETH transfer logs over public RPC. Internal ETH transfers and wallet activity counts come from Blockscout.
  • Ansem's post: x.com/blknoiz06. Lookonchain posts at 09:39, 09:55 and 14:08.
  • Bullpen's statement and MLM's quotes: Decrypt. Bridgers route first reported by Bankless.
  • Relay solver label for 0xf70d…3dbef: Etherscan.
This is a trace of public blockchain data, not an accusation. Wallet ownership beyond public labels is not established. Both end balances were last checked on 26 November 2026 (43.87 BTC and 951.79 ETH, unmoved) and may have changed since.
